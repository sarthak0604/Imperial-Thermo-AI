## Overview
In the United States, combustion processes contribute to about 85% of delivered energy and are an integral part in many industrial fabrication processes. Combustion produces greenhouse gases and soot, which contributes to global warming and causes significant health concerns. The Combustion Science Discipline conducts idealized experiments on the ISS where the removal of gravity enables researchers to study details of the combustion processes that cannot be readily studied on the ground. The Space Station environment also provides an important test-bed for studying spacecraft fire risk.

Combustion occurs when fuel and oxygen react to produce carbon dioxide, water and heat. For the foreseeable future, the overwhelming majority of delivered energy in terrestrial applications will be from combustion or other chemically reacting systems (electric power, transportation, glass and steel manufacture). These processes produce some of the most important environmental hazards currently facing humanity (global climate change, acid gas pollution, mercury contamination from coal, wild-land fires).

Despite being the subject of active research for over 80 years, combustion remains one of the most poorly controlled phenomena with significant impact on human health, comfort and safety, because even the simplest combustor (e.g., a kitchen stove) is beyond our detailed numerical modeling capabilities. Typically hundreds of chemical species and even thousands of reactions are involved; these determine flammability limits (combustor operating ranges) and pollutant emissions. In bench-scale experiments on Earth, buoyant forces created by gravity and the large temperature differential between flame and ambient gas dominate and frequently obscure the phenomena of interest. In the absence of gravity, buoyancy can be suppressed and analyses reduced to much simpler one-dimensional systems.

Typical flame temperatures are on the order of 2,300 K vs ambient ~300 K, producing an eight-fold density change over the scale of a centimeter. The resulting density gradient induces a strong velocity field that dominates all but the highest flow fields, making the flame lift and point upward; resulting flow instabilities cause the flicker typical of flames on Earth. ## Current Combustion Experiments
- **Flammability of Materials on the Moon (FM2)** — first-ever combustion experiment on another planetary body; investigates burning materials on the Moon at 1/6 g, building on ground (1g) and ISS microgravity (~0g) campaigns. Image caption: edge view of Solid Inflammability Boundary at Low-Speed (SIBAL) fabric, a cotton-fiberglass blend, burning in air in lunar gravity (simulated in a drop-tower centrifuge).
- **Solid Fuel Ignition and Extinction investigation (SoFIE)** — Fire is a catastrophic hazard for crewed spacecraft. NASA mitigates risk via NASA-STD-6001 (requirements for evaluation, testing and selection of materials re: flammability, odor, offgassing, fluid compatibility). NASA-STD-6001 implements a 1-g flame propagation test for proposed spaceflight materials which some researchers believe is not conservative in low-g. (Caption: ESA astronaut Samantha Cristoforetti reconfiguring components for SoFIE in the Unity module during Expedition 67.)
- **Flammability Limits At Reduced-g Experiment (FLARE)** — develop a methodology to correlate material flammability limits in normal gravity and microgravity, allowing quantitative estimation of microgravity flammability limits from ground data. International team: JAXA, NASA, ESA, CNES, DLR and universities in Japan, France, Germany and USA.

## Archived Combustion Experiments
- **ACME** — advanced combustion technology via fundamental microgravity research; goal: improve efficiency and reduce pollutant emission in practical terrestrial combustion.
- **BASS** — burning and extinction characteristics of a wide variety of fuel samples in microgravity.
- **DAFT** — ensure the modified P-Trak (key component of SAME) performs properly in microgravity.
- **FLEX** — assess/quantify effectiveness of inert-gas suppressants in microgravity; obtain the most conservative estimate of the limiting oxygen index for steady combustion.
- **Saffire (Spacecraft Fire Safety Demonstration)** — creating a safe environment for astronauts is the number one priority for all crew missions.
- **SAME** — spacecraft smoke detectors must detect different types of smoke; hydrocarbon fuels typically produce soot, plastics produce droplets of recondensed polymer fragments.
- **SLICE** — coflow laminar diffusion flames are valuable because accurate numerical modeling exists for that configuration.
- **SPICE** — continues study of mechanisms controlling stability and extinction of jet diffusion flames begun with Laminar Soot Processes (LSP) on STS-94.

## Facilities on the ISS
- **Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR)** — in the US Lab (Destiny); main feature a 100-liter combustion chamber; the only ISS rack dedicated to combustion; developed by Glenn; launched Nov 2008 on STS-126.
- **Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA)** — multi-user facility for droplet combustion experiments using the CIR in Glenn's Fluids and Combustion Facility (FCF); developed by Glenn; launched Nov 2008.
