## PSI-117 — Effect of External Thermo-Convective Perturbation on Cool Flame Dynamics: A Multidimensional Multi-Physics CFD Analysis

- **Project Type:** Ground Investigation
- **Research Area:** Combustion Science
- **SubResearch Area:** Droplet
- **Related Investigations:** PSI-69     PSI-142
- **Keywords:** Cool Flames     thermo-convective perturbation     Droplet combustion     FLEX
- **Investigation Start Date:** 02-15-2017
- **Investigation End Date:** 02-14-2022
- **Public Release Date:** 2026-06-10
- **Sponsoring Agency:** National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)  PSI grant
- **Monitoring Center:** Glenn Research Center (GRC)
- **Funding Source:** NNX17AF97A
- **Data Source Type:** psi
- **Person:** Farouk Hicks Tanvir Mike
- **Proposal Title:** Effect of External Thermo-Convective Perturbation on Cool Flame Dynamics: A Multidimensional Multi-Physics CFD Analysis

**Objectives**

The objective of this ground investigation was to use flight data obtained from PSI through NASA’s Flame Extinguishment Experiment (FLEX) to quantify how externally imposed thermal and convective perturbations regulated the dynamics and ultimate fate of already established droplet cool flames, and to explain run-to-run variability by isolating the roles of deliberate heating and unintentional drift-induced convection. Focusing on both small and large droplets, the work mapped stability windows for ignition, sustained oscillation, transitions to hot ignition and warm-flame behavior, and extinction under microgravity. By leveraging and extending the FLEX data in the PSI, this study produced reusable models and sensitivity analyses that coupled low-temperature chemistry with transport and radiative effects.

**Approach**

OpenFOAM was used to develop a 3-D CFD model of n-alkane droplet cool flames in microgravity, coupling variable-density Navier–Stokes flow with low-temperature combustion chemistry, coupled heat and mass transfer, and radiation. Model fidelity was established against ISS FLEX measurements, after which controlled simulations examined three forcing modes (i) convection alone to mimic droplet drift, (ii) imposed heat flux, and (iii) combined convection plus heating applied to both large (1–4 mm) and sub-millimeter (~0.5 mm) droplets.

To accelerate progress, the study began with simplified chemistry and transitioned to reduced mechanisms for parameter sweeps over forcing amplitude and frequency, ambient composition (including ozone-assisted cases), and pressure. From each case, predictive observables were extracted (ignition delay, oscillation frequency and amplitude, stand-off and extinction diameters), and were used to construct stability maps that delineated conditions for cool-flame onset, persistence, and quenching. These maps were reconciled with FLEX observations and were used to guide next-generation test matrices for both large- and small-droplet experiments.

**Hypothesis**

External thermo-convective perturbations (imposed temperature gradients, non-uniform heating, oscillatory/shear inflow) were expected to govern cool-flame ignition, oscillation, transition to hot ignition, and extinction by shifting residence time and scalar dissipation. In microgravity, where buoyancy was suppressed, this forcing was anticipated to dominate transport. A multidimensional, multi-physics CFD model was used to map perturbation-dependent stability boundaries and predict observables for comparison with FLEX data.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

Space Benefits: Potential to improve spacecraft fire-safety modeling by resolving cool-flame/extinction behavior in microgravity; leverages FLEX to define safer operating envelopes/materials.
Earth Benefits: Advances low-temperature combustion understanding relevant to engine knock, efficiency, and emissions; delivers validated CFD tools transferrable to practical combustors.

## PSI-159 — Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments / Cool Flames Investigation with Gases

- **Acronym:** ACME CFI-G
- **Project Type:** Microgravity Investigation
- **Research Area:** Combustion Science
- **SubResearch Area:** Flames of Gaseous Fuels
- **Flight Platform:** International Space Station (ISS)
- **Experiment Hardware:** ACME  Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/space/iss-research/iss-fcf/combustion-science/acme/implementation/  https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/space/iss-research/iss-fcf/cir/
- **Keywords:** chemical kinetics     ethane     heat loss     low-temperature combustion     porous burner     dilution     formaldehyde     intensified video camera     n-butane     negative-temperature coefficient
- **Public Release Date:** 2025-11-20
- **Sponsoring Agency:** National Science Foundation (NSF)  Center for the Advancement of Science in Space (CASIS)
- **Monitoring Center:** Glenn Research Center (GRC)
- **Data Source Type:** psi
- **Person:** Stocker Sunderland Axelbaum Williams P. B. L. Dennis Peter Richard Forman
- **Proposal Title:** ISS: Collaborative Research: Spherical Cool Diffusion Flames Burning Gaseous Fuels

**Objectives**

1. Plan and support flight testing on the International Space Station (ISS).
2. Determine the conditions for which cool diffusion flames occur for propane, n-butane, and n-pentane; normal and inverse flames; and a broad range of Zst, Tad, and fluent flow rate.
3. Support the flight tests with advanced CFD simulations.
4. Identify or develop a chemical kinetics mechanism that yields good agreement between the CFD predictions and the ISS measurements.
5. Disseminate the results as widely as possible.

**Approach**

Perform spherical diffusion flame tests aboard the International Space Station. Consider multiple fuels, a range of reactant dilutions, flow rates, and pressures for normal and inverse convection directions. Observe the flames with a broad suite of cameras, radiometers, photomultiplier tubes, and thermocouples.

**Hypothesis**

The hypotheses of this research are that testing aboard the ISS can demonstrate the following for the first time, contributing to an improved understanding of cool flame kinetics.
  1. Steady-state cool diffusion flames on spherical porous burners.
  2. Cool diffusion flames burning propane, n-butane, and n-pentane.
  3. Cool diffusion flames for a wide range of Zst, Tad, and pressures.
  4. Inverse cool diffusion flames.
  5. Cool diffusion flames for which a decrease in fluent flow rate will cause extinction, whereas an increase will cause a transition to a hot diffusion flame, with oscillation possible between hot and cool flames.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

Combustion provides 86% of the world’s energy and an even higher fraction of the energy used for transportation. The internal combustion engines used for transportation generally have thermal efficiencies of 38% or lower because of thermal losses. These efficiencies may increase to 60% if future engines can take advantage of ultra-lean combustion. However, this progress is being impeded by the limited understanding of the chemistry of cool flames. Research into cool diffusion flames (whose peak temperatures are 500 – 1000 K) has grown exponentially since they were discovered in 2012.  The measurements performed for this project will help to test and advance cool flame kinetics mechanisms. This should facilitate the development of more efficient internal combustion engines.

- **Proposal Title:** Flame Design: A Novel Approach to Clean Efficient Diffusion Flames

**Objectives**

The primary objectives of this flight experiment are:
A. Obtain soot-inception limits of quasi-steady normal and inverse spherical diffusion flames as functions of flow rate, Zst, amount of inert (N2 or CO2), and pressure for C2H4 and CH4 flames. Identify the corresponding temperatures in hot regions. Obtain similar limits and temperatures for inverse and normal coflow flames.
B. Obtain detailed measurements in quasi-steady normal and inverse spherical diffusion flames for C2H4 and CH4. Determine the effects of flow rate, Zst, amount of N2, and pressure on flame temperature, size, color, and soot volume fraction. Evaluate the possible existence of steady flames.
C. Obtain extinction limits as functions of Zst, amount of N2, and pressure for normal and inverse spherical C2H4 and CH4 flames. Identify the corresponding temperatures in hot regions. Identify the presence of radiative or kinetic extinction. Evaluate whether pseudo-flammability limits can be obtained for these flames.

**Approach**

Perform spherical diffusion flame tests in drop towers and aboard the International Space Station. Consider a broad range of reactant dilutions, flow rates and pressures for normal and inverse convection directions. Observe the flames with a broad suite of cameras, radiometers, photomultiplier tubes, and thermocouples.

**Hypothesis**

1. Soot formation requires a region where local C/O ratio and T exceed their critical values, and where strain rate is sufficiently low.
2. Microgravity spherical diffusion flames extinguish when the peak temperature first decreases below 1130 K.
3. Steady-state spherical diffusion flames are possible.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

This project has the potential for improving efficiency and reducing pollutant emissions in terrestrial combustors. For example, it is contributing to novel designs of future coal power plants.

## PSI-62 — Concurrent Flame Spread Modeling using Flamelet Generated Manifolds in Microgravity with Comparison to BASS Experiments using Two-Color Tomography

- **Project Type:** Ground Investigation
- **Research Area:** Combustion Science
- **SubResearch Area:** Spacecraft Fire Safety
- **Related Investigations:** PSI-25     PSI-26
- **Keywords:** BASS     Flamelet Generated Manifolds     Flame Spread
- **Investigation Start Date:** 05-01-2019
- **Investigation End Date:** 04-30-2021
- **Public Release Date:** 2025-11-14
- **Sponsoring Agency:** NASA  PSI grant
- **Monitoring Center:** Glenn Research Center (GRC)
- **Funding Source:** 80NSSC20K0426
- **Data Source Type:** psi
- **Person:** DesJardin Urban Paul David
- **Proposal Title:** Concurrent Flame Spread Modeling using Flamelet Generated Manifolds in Microgravity with Comparison to BASS Experiments using Two-Color Tomography

**Objectives**

The objective of this research was to develop and validate new flamelet generated manifold (FGM) modeling techniques for concurrent flame spread in microgravity. Concurrent flame spread presented one of the greatest modeling challenges in combustion science due to the intricate coupling between heat transfer, mass transport, and chemical kinetics near the solid-vapor interface. Traditional modeling strategies often relied on hybridized turbulence and chemistry models that were not fully consistent and failed to capture important intermediate reaction pathways.
To overcome these shortcomings, this PSI grant advanced the use of an unsteady flamelet generated manifold (UFGM) framework that reduced the complexity of reacting systems by mapping them into lower-dimensional manifolds. The research aimed to leverage this methodology to simulate solid fuel combustion processes with high fidelity, using the NASA Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS and BASS-II) experiments as benchmarks. Ultimately, the long-term goal was to enable accurate prediction of flammability limits for spacecraft materials and to provide a tool that could also serve as a subgrid-scale model in large-eddy simulations of fires, supporting spacecraft safety and hazard mitigation.

**Approach**

The approach combined high-resolution computational modeling with experimental validation to study concurrent flame spread in microgravity. A previously developed computational framework  was employed to run fully coupled simulations of fluid–solid responses, focusing on materials subject to charring and ablation. Within this framework, the unsteady FGM method was implemented and tested on various BASS geometries, including flat sheets, spheres, and rod-shaped polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) samples, as well as wax cylinders.
Experimental imagery found in PSI from the BASS and BASS-II investigations was post-processed using newly developed two-color tomography methods with DSLR cameras, enabling 3D reconstructions of soot and temperature fields for direct comparison to model predictions. The evaluation metrics included flame geometry, temperature distribution, soot volume fraction, and flame spread rate, with special emphasis on reproducing the “Goldilocks” flammability zone reported by Olson and Ferkul. Comparative analyses were also carried out with a parallel NSF-supported terrestrial flame spread project, ensuring that the predictive capabilities of the UFGM approach were assessed across both microgravity and normal-gravity conditions.

**Hypothesis**

The unsteady flamelet generated manifold (UFGM) approach was believed to be able to accurately reproduce concurrent flame spread in microgravity, including phenomena such as the “Goldilocks” flammability zone, by coupling near-wall thermal, mass transport, and chemical processes with far-field flow dynamics.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

This research provides predictive tools for screening new materials’ flammability limits, which can lead to the design of safer spacecrafts. The development of Large Eddy Simulation subgrid models is expected to improve hazard assessment capabilities critical for managing fire risks during long-duration space missions and within confined spacecraft habitats. On Earth, these advancements could improve combustion modeling by deepening our understanding of coupled thermal, chemical, and flow processes, which betters fire safety standards. The computational methods being refined have the potential to enable more effective fire prevention and mitigation strategies and  foster innovations in diagnostics and simulations applicable across a variety of combustion scenarios. Overall, this research can contribute significantly to safer human exploration in space and improvements to fire safety protocols on Earth.

## PSI-60 — Computational and Experimental Analysis of SPICE Data Sets

- **Project Type:** Ground Investigation
- **Research Area:** Combustion Science
- **SubResearch Area:** Gaseous
- **Related Investigations:** PSI-107
- **Keywords:** SPICE     Soot     Coflow     Flames     Laminar diffusion flames
- **Investigation Start Date:** 06-05-2019
- **Investigation End Date:** 08-29-2022
- **Public Release Date:** 2025-09-30
- **Sponsoring Agency:** National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)  PSI grant
- **Monitoring Center:** Glenn Research Center (GRC)
- **Funding Source:** 80NSSC19K1634
- **Data Source Type:** psi
- **Person:** Long Smooke Dietrich B. D. L. Marshall Mitchell Daniel
- **Proposal Title:** Computational and Experimental Analysis of SPICE Data Sets

**Objectives**

This ground investigation  sought to increase understanding of the issues that control the large discrepancy between soot formation in 1g and in 0g flames.  The objective of this investigation was to integrate computational and experimental methods to derive temperature distributions and soot volume fractions from the Smoke Point in Coflow Experiment (SPICE) data available in PSI. By analyzing over 1600 raw DSLR images and incorporating advanced soot models, the study sought to improve understanding of soot formation, oxidation, radiative re-absorption, and aggregate growth in heavily sooting flames. The work further aimed to reconcile microgravity and normal gravity flame behavior, identify key physical and chemical processes influencing smoke point development, and provide a framework for evaluating model parameter sensitivity.

**Approach**

The approach  was to combine image processing, experimental diagnostics, and detailed soot modeling. Raw Nikon NEF images were decoded into three color channels and analyzed using color-ratio pyrometry to extract soot temperature fields. Absolute light calibration from SPICE was then used to calculate soot volume fractions. For computational modeling, a coupled flow–species–soot system was implemented, modified to include radiation re-absorption and discrete-sectional aggregate formation. Simulations were performed for both Yale and ACME burner configurations under normal and microgravity conditions. Comparisons between experimental data and computed results guided sensitivity analyses of soot evolution parameters, including the effects of radiation, scattering, boundary conditions, and heat transfer to the burner structure.

**Hypothesis**

Microgravity conditions would produce longer, more diffuse flames with lower temperatures and increased soot concentrations compared to normal gravity, and that careful integration of experimental temperature fields into the simulations would reduce discrepancies between measured and predicted soot distributions.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

This study sought to enhance understanding of soot formation, flame structure, and radiative transport under microgravity and normal gravity, improving predictive models for combustion in both spacecraft and terrestrial systems. **Objectives**

This study addresses the reliability of solder joints in space by pursuing two principal objectives: (1) Characterization of solder-joint formation under microgravity conditions aboard the International Space Station, specifically examining the impact of suppressed convective flow and buoyancy on 40Pb-60Sn alloys utilized in the In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI). (2) Evaluation of the solder’s material and mechanical properties under extreme thermal environments, including elevated and cryogenic temperature exposures characteristic of spaceflight conditions.

**Approach**

The study examined three distinct solder alloys: the ISSI lead-based (40Pb-60Sn) composition, an off-eutectic 50Pb-50Sn formulation, and a lead-free alloy consisting of 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu.
Microstructural characterization was performed on four solder specimens processed in microgravity from the ISSI investigation, one terrestrial ISSI solder, and a freshly fabricated terrestrial 40wt%Pb-60wt%Sn sample. Evidence of void-induced Marangoni convection influencing microstructure development was demonstrated using wire-feed solder processed in space. **Hypothesis**

This suppression of fluid motion results in diminished mixing, causing voids and gas bubbles to remain entrapped within the solder joint during the solidification process. Such internal porosity critically impairs the solder’s thermal and electrical conductivity, while also compromising the mechanical robustness of the joint. To advance the feasibility of extended space missions, this study seeks to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms, observable phenomena, and process parameters that influence the structural and functional integrity of solder joints fabricated under terrestrial and microgravity conditions.

**Objectives**

The main objectives of STDCE-1 are to investigate:
    -The extent and nature of thermocapillary flows in transient periods as well as under steady conditions.
    -Temperature distributions along the free surface and in the bulk fluid.
    -The effect of the heating mode and the heating level on the flows.
    -The effect of the liquid free surface on the flows
    -The onset conditions and nature of oscillatory flows

**Approach**

The STDCE program employed a comprehensive and multifaceted approach to investigate the complexities of thermocapillary flow. This strategy encompassed a combination of theoretical analysis, numerical modeling, ground-based experiments, and ultimately, microgravity experiments aboard the Space Shuttle.

Theoretical Analysis
   Dimensionless Parameters: Researchers derived and identified the key dimensionless parameters governing thermocapillary flow, including the Marangoni number (Ma), Prandtl number (Pr), aspect ratio (Ar), and the novel surface deformation parameter (S). These parameters facilitated the understanding of the relative importance of different forces and physical mechanisms at play and provided a framework for data analysis and comparison.

   Scaling Laws: By analyzing the governing equations and boundary conditions, scientists established scaling laws that characterized the behavior of key flow features, such as boundary layer thicknesses and velocity scales. This theoretical groundwork offered valuable insights into the expected flow characteristics under different conditions.

Numerical Modeling
   Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD): Sophisticated CFD models were developed to simulate thermocapillary flow behavior under various conditions. These models accounted for factors such as heat transfer, fluid properties, surface tension effects, and free surface deformation. The numerical simulations provided detailed predictions of flow patterns, velocity distributions, and temperature profiles, serving as a basis for comparison with experimental data and for exploring a wider range of parameters than feasible experimentally.

Ground-Based Experiments
   Reduced-Gravity Simulations: Researchers conducted experiments in drop towers and other reduced-gravity facilities to observe thermocapillary flows under conditions with minimized buoyancy effects. These experiments served as a stepping stone towards the microgravity experiments and provided preliminary data for model validation and refinement.

   Test Fluid and Hardware Development: Extensive ground-based testing was undertaken to optimize the experimental setup, including the selection of the test fluid (silicone oil), the design of the test cell, and the development of flow visualization and temperature measurement techniques.

Microgravity Experiments
   Space Shuttle Missions: The culmination of the research effort involved conducting the STDCE experiments aboard the Space Shuttle, taking advantage of the extended microgravity environment to study thermocapillary flows over a broader range of parameters and conditions than possible on Earth. This included meticulous execution of the test matrix, encompassing various heating modes, power levels, and free surface shapes, while collecting detailed flow visualization and temperature data.

Data Analysis and Model Validation
   Comparison of Results: The experimental data from both ground-based and microgravity experiments were rigorously analyzed and compared with the predictions from numerical models. This iterative process allowed for the assessment of model accuracy, the identification of areas for improvement, and ultimately, a deeper understanding of the fundamental physics driving thermocapillary flow.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

While the STDCE program was primarily driven by the need to understand and predict thermocapillary flow behavior in microgravity environments, its findings and technological advancements have yielded valuable insights and applications for Earth-based processes as well.

Fundamental Physics: STDCE significantly advanced the understanding of the fundamental physics governing thermocapillary flows, including the interplay between surface tension gradients, heat transfer, and fluid motion. This knowledge provides a solid foundation for tackling complex fluid flow problems in various terrestrial applications.

**Approach**

The Saffire-1 experiments was performed on the Orbital Science’s Cygnus resupply vehicle after it deberthed from the ISS. The sample material for Saffire-I is a composite fabric called Sibal consisting of cotton on a fiberglass substrate (75% cotton, 25% fiberglass by weight). There is a significant lack of microgravity data available for spacecraft designers on which to base their fire safety designs and procedures. To address this knowledge gap, this investigation proposed to conduct large-scale fire safety experiments in an expendable spacecraft without risk to a crew.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

**Objectives**

One of the major goals of this investigation was to quantify the sizes that the liquid smoke particulate achieves in low gravity and to relate the droplet size distribution to the smoke generation and smoke transport conditions in the experiment. A numerical code was to be developed to predict the smoke droplet growth as a function of the fuel pyrolysis rate, the thermodynamic properties of the pyrolysis vapor, and the flow environment.

**Approach**

The crew installed the near field hardware in the Glovebox, attached the Far Field Box to the Glovebox, and positioned the video cameras. The operator then ran the self diagnostic procedures on the two smoke detectors and activated the video cameras, turned on the igniter for a predefined period of time (15 to 60 seconds), and initiated the thermophoretic soot samplers to sample the smoke. The actual duration of each combustion event was typically 2 minutes. After flight, the particulate collected on the thermophoretic probes were analyzed using a Transmission Electron microscope (TEM) to determine primary and aggregate particle dimensions. The remains of the samples were weighed to determine their mass loss. The digital data was transcribed from the video record and analyzed to determine the response of the detectors. Once the flight mass loss rates were determined, 1-g operation conditions to produce the same loss were determined.The measurements were made using smoke generated by overheated  spacecraft materials where variables such as the sample temperature, flow field, and particle aging time were more rigorously controlled.

**Hypothesis**

The three hypotheses that motivated this investigation:
In a μg environment, with the absence of the strong buoyancy-induced flows, smoke particle growth from a pyrolyzing/smoldering source, as a result of nucleation, condensation, and coagulation, will lead to larger particles compared to those produced in 1g. The formation of the larger particles is favored by the expected higher vapor concentration and smoke residence times near the source due to the reduced velocities and mixing at μg conditions. In particular, it is hypothesized that liquid aerosol smokes produced in microgravity will exhibit substantially larger droplet sizes than under the same conditions in normal gravity (with a corresponding decrease in the particle number density for the same mass of smoke). The change in the size distribution and number density will, in turn, affect the response of the smoke detectors and possibly result in some detectors not producing an alarm when exposed to this smoke.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

The SAME experiment will provide technology for an advanced fire detector for future spacecraft that will be used for long duration missions. SAME will provide quantitative data on the sensitivity of these detectors to reduced gravity smokes that will allow evaluation of the adequacy of these existing technologies using relevant data. The current Fire Prevention, Detection, and Suppression (FPDS) program plan allows for the re-evaluation of future sensor technology, to allow new technology and capability to be utilized. The results from SAME are needed to provide the reduced gravity baseline data against which future detection technology developments can be evaluated.The smoke detectors developed from the results of SAME can also be useful in other extreme environments on Earth, such as submarines or underwater laboratories. Accurate detection of smoke in these environments can save lives.

**Objectives**

The BRE flight program seeks to establish the burning conditions in a quiescent microgravity ambient in terms of material properties.
The specific flight objectives are as follows.
  1. Observe burning behavior in long-duration microgravity for CH4/N2 and C2H4/N2mixtures on porous circular burners of 25  and 50 mm diameter. Consider O2/N2 ambients with various pressures and ambient O2 concentrations and pressures. NASA - considered atmosphere for human space missions will be examined: 14.7 psia, 21 % oxygen: 10.2 psia, 26.5 %; and 8.2 psia, 34 % oxygen. Emphasize fuel conditions that can be related to a diverse range of condensed fuels.
  2. Consider the conditions of Objective 1 for ambients of O2/CO2 (desired).
  3. Consider the conditions of Objectives 1 – 2 for extinction limits.

**Approach**

To accomplish the above objectives, flight experiments are proposed. Additionally, preliminary flight tests will be performed to verify the experiment operations and for comparison with ground-based tests.

For each test, conditions will be established for the chamber contents, namely choice of diluent, oxygen concentration, and pressure. Fuel flow will commence followed by ignition. The flames will initially be observed without varying the fuel flow rate. Because burner surface temperature and heat flux will vary, this will allow a broad range of conditions to be observed during a single test. As the surface temperature of the burner increases, and the flame remains steady, a continuous set of data is produced for the flammability map. Eventually either the flame will extinguish, the surface temperature of the burner will equilibrate, or the temperature limit of the heat flux sensor will be reached. At this time the fuel flow will be terminated. So a test should terminate for a given flow rate at extinction, and for continued burning when the burner surface temperature has steadied or the temperature limit of the heat flux sensor is neared.

The extinction limit could be found once the burner temperature has steadied, and the heat flux sensor has not exceeded its limit. At that state, the flow rate can be reduced in small increments, until extinction is observed and the flow rate shut off. Again, in this process, new data is being produced for the flammability map. Ignition limit tests are not proposed for microgravity, but we will explore these under normal gravity. We believe there is better control on Earth-based ignition testing and that these results should carry over to microgravity conditions.

In addition to the flight experiments, in normal gravity testing will continue. These tests will involve gaseous, liquid, and solid fuels. These experiments will further establish the validation of the BRE for 1-g applications and show its usefulness to establish 1-g ignition and extinction limits. As available, drop facility testing will be used to refine procedures, test instrumentation, and help to establish viable flow conditions.

We will also pursue a CFD model for the quiescent microgravity burning. This will also guide us in the flight experiments, and potentially provide a predictive methodology.

**Hypothesis**

The BRE investigation hypothesizes that there can exist a regime of quasi-steady burning in microgravity as a function of materials properties. Steady burning may never be achieved, and only slowly changing unsteady flames are possible. The proposed tests aboard the ISS aims to query this hypothesis by establishing a range of quasi-steady burning conditions for condensed fuels in the microgravity environment. It is hypothesized that BRE can determine a range of burning conditions in microgravity as a function of heat of gasification, reradiation heat flux, applied radiant heat flux, heat of combustion and smoke point. This could enable the Cone Calorimeter (NASA Test 2) test as a basis for establishing flammability maps for real materials.

The key hypothesis is that the heat of combustion, smoke point, and heat of gasification of the fuel mixture will represent a real solid fuel (or liquid fuel absorbed in an inert matrix). Thus achieved steady burning would represent the potential for real solid fuels to achieve steady burning in microgravity. Other than a candle flame, this has not been shown before.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

BRE yielded important data relevant to spacecraft fire safety. Quasi-steady burning corresponding to various condensed-phase materials (such as PMMA and Nylon) was observed in microgravity despite the absence of air flow, where the cessation of ventilation is an established step in putting out spacecraft fires. The ongoing combustion without air flow was notably seen in elevated-oxygen atmospheres which are being considered for use in future spacecraft, whereas such flames in a normal atmosphere of 21% oxygen self-extinguished in a short time. While BRE’s results are still preliminary, they suggest that NASA may want to increase use of its alternative flammability Test 2, with the cone calorimeter, where that test can measure material properties corresponding to the microgravity flames observed with the BRE burner.

- **Proposal Title:** The Fundamental Studies in Droplet Combustion and Flame Extinguishment in Microgravity

**Objectives**

The primary goal of the FLEX-2 flight experiments is to extend the results of FLEX-1 to fuels and environmental conditions that mimic real combustor conditions, as well as, provide benchmark experimental data on bi-component droplet combustion over a wide range of initial conditions in order to validate numerical simulations and to develop new theoretical models.

Detailed experimental objectives can be further itemized as:
  1. Investigate the role of liquid-phase mixing on the burning characteristics of bi-component fuel droplets.
  2. Examine the steady and unsteady liquid and gas-phase phenomena in pure fuel combustion.
  3. Study flame extinction mechanisms and their boundaries.
  4. Analyze the soot formation process, including soot volume fraction soot-shell dynamics.
  5. Determine the influence of droplet interactions on the flammability limits and extinction behavior of droplet arrays.
  6. Validate theoretical models of droplet combustion through comprehensive experimental data collection in microgravity environments.

**Approach**

The chamber was filled with the appropriate atmosphere, which was varied in pressure between 0.5 atm to 3.0 atm and O2 concentration between 0.1 to 0.4 mole fraction with the diluent gas making up the balance. A settling time of approximately 2 minutes  elapsed prior to initiating the test in order to ensure that the temperature and pressure of the chamber gases have stabilized. This settling time was followed by the dispensing of a predetermined amount of fuel (based on the target droplet size) onto the support fiber for those tests in which the droplet is not freely deployed. When sufficient fuel has been dispensed the dispensing needles were retracted and a dwell period of at least 10 seconds was allowed for the droplet internal fluid motion induced by deployment to subside. This was then followed by initiating power to the ignitor for a selectable amount of time ranging from 1 second to 5 seconds after which the ignitor was retracted from the field of view. If the flow field is to be generated by translating the droplet then droplet motion would commence at the same time that the ignitor is retracted. A near real-time download of the color camera video was employed in order to verify successful droplet deployment, ignition, and overall progress of the experiment. Pressure and temperature data of the chamber environment was also recorded in near real time.

The overall investigation approach can be summarized as:
  1. Test parameters are determined, including pressure, temperature, fuel mixture, and humidity.
  2. Fill the combustion chamber with the selected gas mixtures.
  3. Deploy the droplet(s) carefully controlling the droplet diameter.
  4. Allow the droplet to stabilize in the microgravity environment.
  5. Ignite the droplet in a uniform and controlled fashion.
  6. Monitor combustions and track flame evolution using high speed photography.
  7. Monitor and adjust experimental conditions as needed.
  8. Analyze data.

The independently varied experimental parameters during FLEX-2 experiments were 1) Fuel type 2) Ambient gas composition 3) Ambient Pressure 4) Droplet initial diameter 5) Droplet translation velocity (and acceleration) 6) Droplet spacing and initial size in a binary linear array.

**Hypothesis**

Fairly sophisticated numerical simulations of convective droplet combustion have been developed in the last decade where the key contribution is the inclusion of full Navier-Stokes equations for the gas-phase flow field. However, due to the inherent complexity of the problem, varying degrees of approximations have been made in treating the chemistry, radiation, and the coupling between the gas and liquid phases. The validity of the assumptions or the hypothesis used in these simulation have not been confirmed by using benchmark data. By conducting these experiments in a microgravity environment, the effects of buoyancy are minimized, and the droplet can be characterized only as a function of thermocapillary or solutalcapillary stresses allowing for a clear observation of the fundamental combustion processes,  The present FLEX-2 experiments using bi-component mixtures. **Objectives**

The objective of this investigation is to better understand the relationship between electric field voltage and chemi-ion current, analyze the dynamic response of flames to electric field changes, evaluate soot formation, and characterize stabilized near-limit lifted flames.

**Approach**

The E-FIELD flames experiment investigates how electric fields influence the behavior of laminar diffusion flames in microgravity. This unique environment is ideal for isolating electric field effects from buoyancy forces.
The experiment is conducted with gas-fueled flames, both with and without a coflow of air. In the former case, the tests are conducted using the same coflow burner used by the Coflow Laminar Diffusion (CLD) Flame experiment. An electric field is generated by creating a high voltage (up to 10 kV) differential between the burner and a flat circular mesh suspended above (i.e., downstream of) the burner. Measurements, as a function of the field strength and fuel, are taken of the ion current through the flame and the flame’s response time to electric forcing. The gas-jet burner is used to create a small, focused flame for detailed ion-driven convection studies while the coflow burner surrounds the fuel jet with a weak synthetic air flow to enable flame stability and soot behavior analysis. A high-voltage copper mesh electrode positioned above the burner tip generated an electric field with controlled polarity and strength. The electric field interacts with ions generated through chemi-ionization during combustion, driving ion-driving convection and influencing flame behavior. Methane and ethylene were used as fuels and diluted with nitrogen to control flame strength. Dilution levels were varied to explore a range of combustion dynamics, from highly stable to near-extinction flames. Voltage-current curves were recorded to assess the relationship between electric field strength and chemi-ion current, revealing ion-driven convection effects. High-resolution cameras captured flame shape, luminosity, and soot distribution. Spectral imaging with filters measures chemiluminescent emissions and soot volume fraction. There were thermopile detectors to measure flame radiant emissions.
All experiments were conducted in the combustion integrated rack (CIR). Tests were administered with Glenn Research Center (GRC) directing astronaut actions. Parameters such as fuel flow rate, coflow velocity, and field polarity are were varied to isolate specific effects on ion production, flame stability, and soot behavior.

**Hypothesis**

The application of electric fields in microgravity environments will significantly influence laminar diffusion flames by enabling precise manipulation of flame stability, soot behavior, and combustion dynamics through ion-driven convection and chemi-ionization processes.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

The E-FIELD Flames research is not being conducted to serve any space applications, but it is possible that its findings could aid the development of future space-based combustion devices (e.g., for solid waste processing). The use of electric fields to control flames can reduce the production of harmful pollutants in practical terrestrial combustion. The combustion of fossil fuel is the primary human source of greenhouse gas and acid rain and furthermore creates soot, which can be beneficial but is also a health hazard. Combustion using electrical fields may also allow for more efficient combustion, reducing fuel consumption in addition to unwanted emissions.

**Objectives**

A series of experiments is proposed with the following specific objectives, in both soot-free and sooty flames, to compare with detailed computational simulation to assess transport properties and detailed chemical kinetics (including chemiluminescence):
    Objective A. Measure and characterize the transient structure (such as temperature and species distribution) of diffusion flames established at initially non-steady, spherical flame structures for mixtures of different H2/CH4/diluent ratios   (soot-free) and different C2H4/diluent ratios (sooty), for different flow rates.
    Objective B. Determine the quasi-steady convective/chemical-kinetic extinction limits (low system Damköhler numbers) and radiative/chemical-kinetic extinction limits (high system Damköhler numbers) for mixtures of different H2/CH4/diluent ratios (soot-free) and different C2H4/diluent ratios (sooty).

**Approach**

A series of experiments is proposed in which mixtures of various gaseous fuels and diluents will be issued into an oxidizing environment through a spherical porous burner and ignited to obtain, nominally, a spherical, one-dimensional stabilized flame. Conventional gaseous fuels including hydrogen, methane, and ethylene will be used. Hydrogen is the simplest fuel and its reaction mechanism is also reasonably established. Methane is the simplest hydrocarbon and extensive studies have been conducted on its combustion chemistry. Ethylene is a typical sooty fuel and is also an important intermediate for the oxidation of higher hydrocarbons. Diluents mixed with fuel are to include nitrogen and helium to adjust mixture diffusivities, and carbon dioxide to influence flame radiation characteristics.

**Hypothesis**

The transient numerical simulation of spherical flames developed as part of this program includes detailed chemistry and transport models, especially radiation mechanisms. Each description of the flames obtained from the experiment, including measurements of aspects of the flame structure, growth or flame front velocities, and the specific test conditions where steady state flames, flame extinctions, flame front instabilities, soot suppression limits, etc., comprise a rigorous test of the detailed model. Together they provide a measure of the comprehensiveness of the chemical kinetic and transport models.
The numerical simulation is also being used as a predictive tool to develop the science requirements for this experiment, especially the collection of test conditions and most aspects of the measurement systems. Due to time and g-jitter limitations, little of the planned test matrix can be adequately explored in the ground based test facilities, and the flight experiment definition will rely on the best effort in the modeling work.

- **Proposal Title:** Droplet Flame Extinguishment in Microgravity

**Objectives**

The FLEX (Flame Extinguishment Experiment) investigation aims to map the flammability limits of liquid fuels in microgravity environments, measure the effectiveness of various gaseous fire suppressants such as CO2, He, and SF6, and develop reliable predictive models for combustion limits under reduced gravity conditions. The mission seeks to validate and improve simplified models by incorporating detailed chemical reactions, heat transfer, and material movement, using both space-based data and extensive ground-based tests. Key flight experiment goals include obtaining high-resolution measurements of droplet burning rates, flame extinction behavior, heat radiation, soot levels, and temperature profiles, while systematically analyzing the effects of different ambient conditions and suppressant concentrations. These investigations are essential for advancing the fundamental understanding of combustion processes in microgravity and enhancing fire safety protocols for future space missions.

**Approach**

The FLEX experiments involve igniting and observing the burning behavior of relatively large droplets. A primary objective of the experiments is to determine the Limiting Oxygen Index (LOI) as a function of ambient pressure and determine how the presence of a suppressant influences the LOI .
The basic outline of the procedure to construct this diagram is as follows:
  1. Select the test pressure (0.5 − 1.5 atm) for the LOI tests.
  2. Select a smaller droplet size (≈ 3 mm)
  3. Select an ambient oxygen mole fraction that, based on ground-based testing, will support droplets burning to completion (as opposed to extinguishing at a finite droplet size).
  4. Perform a droplet combustion test.
  5. Increase the droplet size or repeat the test (if finite extinction diameter at flame extinction observed).
  6. Continue steps 4 and 5 until flame exists for only a short time after ignition (radiative extinction).
  7. Continue steps 4 thru 6 at a decreased ambient oxygen mole fraction.
  8. Continue step 7 to an ambient oxygen mole fraction where the droplet does not ignite.

There were ambient conditions where the extinction droplet sizes at all ambient oxygen mole fractions were too small to either be observed or measured with the available diagnostics. In this case, the determination of the LOI is similar, but more iterative. Instead of observing flame extinction, however, the procedure  involved an iterative sequence of tests (burn to completion followed by a no ignition test) with progressively smaller increments in ambient oxygen mole fraction to determine the LOI . For the slow flow tests (in the flight experiment), the configuration of the flight hardware did not allow for adequate diagnostics over the entire test for a droplet in a uniform, steady convective flow.  In these tests, the procedure will be to duplicate tests above which yield finite-sized extinction droplet diameters. Near the end of the tests, prior to flame extinction, the droplet will be subjected to a small, sub-buoyant, convective flow (imparted by translating the droplet at a slow, uniform speed).

The goal of the experiment is then to see how the presence of this flow influences the extinction process. Note, a large number of the tests involve free droplets (as opposed to fiber-supported droplets) and a number of these tests will have droplets with a slow drift velocity. Data gathered from these tests to examine the influences of a small, sub-buoyant flow on both the burning and extinction process.

**Hypothesis**

The primary hypothesis of the FLEX mission is that microgravity conditions fundamentally alter the combustion and extinction characteristics of liquid fuel droplets due to changes in species and energy transport dynamics, radiative heat loss, and chemical kinetics. Specifically, the hypothesis posits that the lack of buoyant convection in microgravity will lead to significant differences in the flammability limits and suppression efficacy of various fire suppressant agents compared to normal gravity conditions. This hypothesis underpins the design of the FLEX experiments, which aim to map flammability boundaries, quantify suppressant efficacy, and develop predictive models to improve fire safety in space environments.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

The research impacts and Earth benefits of the FLEX mission are significant and multifaceted. By enhancing our understanding of microgravity combustion, the mission directly contributes to improved fire safety protocols for future space missions, ensuring the safety of crew members in environments where traditional fire suppression methods may be ineffective. On Earth, the insights gained from these experiments have the potential to advance fire safety in confined and reduced-gravity environments, such as submarines or high-altitude aircraft. The mission's findings on the effectiveness of various fire suppressants, including CO2, He, and SF6, in microgravity can lead to the development of more efficient and less harmful fire suppression systems, reducing the risk of toxic byproducts during fire events. Additionally, the study of ''cool flames'' and low-temperature combustion processes observed in microgravity can inform the design of advanced combustion engines, leading to improved fuel efficiency and reduced emissions in automotive and industrial applications. Overall, the FLEX mission's contributions to combustion science have the potential to enhance both space exploration safety and terrestrial fire safety technologies.

**Objectives**

The two main objectives for BASS are to:
    1.) Begin to bridge the gap between the normal gravity NASA-STD-6001 Test#1 method, ground-based microgravity tests, and actual material flammability in microgravity. The relation between these can be investigated by observing flames burning for the longer times available in flight. Practical, realistic (thicker) fuels in typical geometries will be examined, including wake flames which have characteristics of a fire burning behind another object (for example, a fire burning in tight area which may not be easily accessible).
    2.) Assess the effectiveness of an inert, gaseous extinguishing agent (similar to that used on ISS) in putting out flames over different materials, geometries, preheating, and flow.

**Approach**

The experiment was conducted aboard the International Space Station (ISS) inside the Microgravity Glove Box (MSG). Custom-made fabric samples consisting of 75% cotton and 25% fiberglass (by mass) measuring were used as a fuel source as well as Nomex fabric and Ultem. A Kanthal wire (29-gage)igniter was used to initiate combustion at one end of the fabric. Airflow was controlled using a variable speed fan, with airflow speeds reaching as high as 40 cm/s. The airflow was directed along the fuel surface in either the opposed or concurrent direction.

This investigation explored two primary air flow configurations; concurrent-flow and opposed-flow. With concurrent-flow air flows in the same direction as the flame spread and in opposed-flow air flows in the opposite direction of flame spread. Flames were observed in the top and edge profiles and data was collected on flame size, shape, color, spread rate, and potential flame quenching. The samples were placed in a small flow duct (76 mm x 76 mm cross section) that allowed for airflow across the fabric. Other sample shapes were utilized as well including spheres and cylinders.

**Hypothesis**

BASS experiments were based on the hypothesis that materials in microgravity, with adequate ventilation, burn as well if not better than the same material in normal gravity with other conditions being identical (pressure, oxygen concentration, temperature, etc.) To test this hypothesis with any but the thinnest materials requires extended microgravity time – longer than available in drop towers or parabolic aircraft.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

Current NASA spacecraft materials selection is based on a standard test method (NASA–STD–6001 Test 1) that segregates material based on 1-g behavior without consideration of low gravity effects. A critical element of this understanding is the radiative heat emission from the flame. These results are used in first order models and predictions of heat release in spacecraft fires and as a means to extend heat release data from tests like the NASA cone calorimeter test (NASA–STD–6001 Test 2) to a performance-based material selection process. Using nitrogen as a flame suppressant in microgravity provides a direct link to current and planned extinguishment techniques.
BASS results provide essential guidance to ground-based microgravity combustion research efforts. Detailed combustion models are validated using the simpler flow environment afforded by tests in microgravity. Once validated, they can be used to build more complex combustion models needed to capture the important details of flames burning in normal gravity. These models have wide applicability to the general understanding of many terrestrial combustion problems and will guide novel spacecraft design and ensure the safety of future crewed missions.

## PSI-47 — Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test / Dust and Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test-2 (DAFT / DAFT-2)

(Taken from the landing page, version 5, updated 02-Jun-2025; total files 39.83 MB)

**Objectives**

The focus of DAFT / DAFT-2 was to mitigate the risk for SAME. SAME relied on a commercially available condensate nuclei counter, the TSI P-Trak, to quantify smoke aerosols in microgravity, but there were concerns the P-Trak would not be reliable because it relies on gravity to recycle isopropyl alcohol. DAFT/DAFT-2 tests that a modified P-Trak would be accurate in microgravity. Summarized objectives: 1) Demonstrate functionality of the modified TSI P-Trak in microgravity; 2) Compare P-Trak performance against the DustTrak control; 3) Create and analyze aerosol standards in microgravity; 4) Ensure reliability of future aerosol experiments.

**Approach**

1) Instrument modification: condenser tube redesigned to use capillary forces for recycling isopropyl alcohol; mercury orientation switch removed. 2) Aerosol generation: Arizona Red Dust aerosolized with GN2, contained in 15-liter Mylar bags. 3) On-orbit testing: modified P-Trak data compared against the DustTrak CNC designed for microgravity.

**Hypothesis**

P-Trak condenses isopropanol around particulates that are laser-counted. Feared failure modes: (1) clogging/obscuring by isopropanol; (2) internal airflow dynamics of the optics chamber causing over-counting. Hypotheses: (1) the modified P-Trak functions normally in microgravity; (2) the aerosol created and filtered per design is within the devices' measurement capability.

**Research Impacts/Earth Benefits**

Extended-duration missions with larger vessels need fire detection with higher reliability and sensitivity; informs novel ISS/spacecraft smoke detection and instruments for other challenging Earth environments such as submarines or underwater laboratories.
